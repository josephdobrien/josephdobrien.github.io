About Me

I am currently a Ph.D. candidate at UCSD working primarily in the philosophy of science and epistemology. My research is focused on the development of machine learning (ML) systems and their impact on the sciences. In one branch of my work, I explore how scientific communities interact with algorithmic tools, and how these tools affect the dynamics of knowledge production. The other branch focuses on how these algorithmic tools bear on traditional problems in the philosophy of science, such as scientific representation, confirmation, and modeling. Much of my work draws on methods and insights from computer science, statistics, network science, and machine learning theory. Prior to philosophy, I did research in computational geography, training deep neural networks (DNNs) to provide insights into environmental and social impacts on communities.

Selected Publications

Navigating Epistemic Monocultures in AI-Driven Science: A Simulation Study

Fazelpour, Sina; O'Brien, Joseph; Rubin, Hannah (2026)

Philosophy of Science

Journal Site
Abstract

AI integration into scientific communities promises accelerated discovery but raises concerns about detrimental homogenization. We develop an NK landscape model to explore these promises and risks.We find that non-personalized AI systems that offer uniform guidance yield benefits only under a narrow conjunction of problem structure, practices, and baseline research capabilities, becoming harmful otherwise. We implement two proposed mitigations: randomization and personalization. While randomization’s utility remains restricted to decomposable problems, personalization can enhance diversity, enabling benefits across a broader range of conditions. Crucially, these benefits are not automatic: they depend on effective institutional adaptation, requiring new standards, protocols, and practices.

A Philosophical Introduction to In-Silico Clinical Trials

O'Brien, Joseph; Masotti, Joseph; Cartwright, Nancy (2026)

European Journal for Philosophy of Science

Journal Site Preprint
Abstract

In-silico clinical trials (ISCTs) for medical devices and products are computer-based simulations of material clinical trials, often of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) on populations of ‘virtual’ patients. A major aim for developing ISCTs is to use them to complement (or potentially sometimes replace) material trials for providing evidence about the performance of medical products. While these methods are beginning to see use, especially as additional sources of evidence that regulatory agencies allow, they remain relatively under-theorized by philosophers of science. In this paper, we aim to introduce philosophers of science to ISCTs, focusing on ISCTs that generate results using knowledge-based models. Although we identify a number of areas in philosophy of science that we expect can make significant contributions in thinking about ISCTs, from work on measurement and models to that on computer simulations, our own offerings are on issues related to the use of RCTs themselves, like external and internal validity, and on the various regulatory standards for their use. Because of the practical importance of their use as evidence for effectiveness and safety by regulatory agencies, we focus most on how in-silico randomized control trials (ISRCTs) compare with material RCTs done on real patients, which most regulatory agencies see as the gold standard for evidence of intervention effectiveness. Our primary examples are of medical devices since this seems to be the area where suggestions for ISCT use is most developed. Our hope is that this introductory paper will prompt other philosophers of science to delve deeper into this extremely interesting and important area of applied science that can have real practical effects on our lives as ISCT results are increasingly allowed as evidence for medical effectiveness and safety.

A multi-glimpse deep learning architecture to estimate socioeconomic census metrics in the context of extreme scope variance

Runfola, Dan; Stefanidis, Anthony; Lv, Zhonghui; O'Brien, Joseph; Baier, Heather (2024)

International Journal of Geographical Information Science

Journal Site
Abstract

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are leveraged for a wide range of satellite imagery information extraction tasks. However, for tasks which seek to estimate aggregated information across highly variable geographic extents, existing techniques are subject to critical limitations. We engage with a specific case study exploring this challenge: estimating census variables across 2358 Mexican municipalities, which range in scope from 2.21 km2 (~74,000 30 m pixels) to 72,417.9 km2 (millions of pixels). Building on recent literature which has illustrated the capability of deep learning to extract socioeconomic information from satellite imagery, we specifically seek to establish baseline metrics of error that might be expected when estimating a range of census variables based on coarse-resolution (Landsat) satellite imagery alone. For each of 52 variables, we implement a multi-glimpse recurrent attention model, in which we parametrically determine subsets of each municipality to sample across iterative steps. Results of a five-fold validation indicate that nearly half of the tested variables (22) can be estimated with r2 values greater than 0.75. Results suggest considerable promise for the use of satellite imagery to estimate socioeconomic factors in both historic time periods for which surveys were not conducted, as well as contemporary inaccessible regions.

Works in Progress (email me for drafts)

The Epistemic Status of Synthetic Data Under Review
Abstract

Research in the computational sciences increasingly relies on tools from machine learning (ML) to analyze large datasets and extract latent patterns that are cognitively inaccessible to human scientists. However, ML tools typically require massive amounts of data to achieve reliable performance, often outrunning the data available in these fields. To compensate for the deficit of available data, researchers have increasingly turned to synthetic data generation as a potential solution. In this paper, using case studies across various scientific fields, I examine the increasing role of synthetic data in the computational sciences and argue that we currently lack an account that characterizes the epistemic value of synthetic data. Drawing on tools from ML theory, I then propose an account of epistemic value in which the value of synthetic data is a function of the structure of the target phenomenon, the model architecture used to generate the data, and the downstream task the data will be used for. As synthetic data is increasingly utilized throughout the computational sciences, properly characterizing the epistemic value of synthetic data is of increased importance for both philosophers of science and practitioners of ML, providing a potential avenue for future research and collaboration in this burgeoning area of applied science.

Signal and Noise in the Sciences Draft in Progress
Abstract

A standard conception of data is that they consist of one or more relatively stable signals that one is trying to extract or model, combined with noise that interferes with that modeling. A significant amount of work in statistical inference aims to formalize our intuitive abilities to distinguish between signal and noise, and develop frameworks to reliably distinguish between signal and noise in a given dataset. Implicit in much statistical practice is the assumption that there is a fact of the matter about what is signal vs. noise. In this paper, however, we argue that this implicit assumption is too simple. We provide a taxonomy of multiple distinct conceptual grounds for distinguishing between signal and noise, explore how these are related, and suggest that the choice of conceptualization - and hence, the choice of statistical method - is a value-laden, pragmatic choice to be made by scientists depending on the use case. That is, we contend that there is not a unique, privileged way to distinguish signal from noise in a dataset, but also that scientists are not free to choose any pattern whatsoever for the signal. There are multiple, but not infinitely many, ways to decompose a dataset into signal and noise, and the choice among those options should depend on the interests, goals, and capabilities of scientists.

Theoretical Virtues and Modern Machine Learning Draft in Progress
Abstract

This paper revisits the classic prediction-accommodation debate in the philosophy of science, which has typically been framed in terms of the temporal relationship between model selection and data collection. In the context of machine learning (ML), I argue that this framing mislocates the source of epistemic credit for modern neural networks. Leveraging research in the foundations of ML, I argue that philosophers ought to eschew the traditional predictivist-accommodationist framing and instead direct their attention to the content of the model's inductive commitments given its implicit regularization structure. What matters is what the model is implicitly committed to, rather than when a specific model is committed to.

Data Leakage and the Independence of Model Benchmarking Draft in Progress
Abstract

Developing modern machine learning models typically involves an iterative two-step process of training and updating model parameters, and evaluating the trained model in an attempt to determine whether the model generalizes to previously unseen data. Data leakage describes scenarios in which information from data used to evaluate the model is present during the training process, which can lead researchers to overestimate model performance. In this paper, I explore the ways that different types of data leakage differentially impact both individual model evaluation and cross-model evaluation via benchmarking tasks. While data leakage is widely acknowledged to impact model evaluation and benchmarking, I argue that even when leakage is guarded against, there is an additional way that the epistemic value of benchmarking can be undermined. This occurs in the presence of inductive basis overlap, which manifests primarily through the shared use of pretrained embeddings. I argue that inductive basis overlap can lead to a novel form of algorithmic monoculture which is invisible to existing auditing frameworks, and show how this form of monoculture can affect scientific research. I close by exploring the connections between these ideas and research on artificial intelligence alignment, arguing that we should broaden the notion of alignment to include mitigation techniques aimed at intervening early in the model development pipeline.
